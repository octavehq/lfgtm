# Digest editorial guidance

For digest work, preserve attribution and the user’s question when applying shared editorial rules. Do not rewrite authored positioning or extraction metadata as buyer behavior to avoid mentioning internal systems: say “our positioning emphasizes…” when that is the source. A routine observation or no material change can answer the question; novelty is not required.

The editorial rules apply to **titles and headings**, not just body copy. Every chapter, section, and spread title must state its finding as an intelligible sentence, or, if it is a short label, be clearly decipherable on its own. A vague evocative fragment that could sit on any report ("The Pressure," "The New Owner," "The Wall") does not pass: lead with the claim it stands in for. State the finding **plainly**. A dramatic reversal or clever turn ("we won X and inherited Y," "we won the argument and lost the war") fails on the opposite end, reading as performative even when the underlying finding is real. The editorial review must audit titles the same way it audits prose.

**Write like a dispatch, not an inside joke.** Frame every finding for a reader who was not on the calls and does not yet share the context. State plainly what happened, to whom, and why it matters before layering in the clever turn. An elliptical, knowing line that assumes shared context ("Buyers stopped asking why and started handing our own framing back to us," "the conversation moved") is a failure even when it is literally true: the reader has to reverse-engineer what it means. A real magazine sets the scene, then delivers the point. Prefer the sentence a smart colleague who missed the week would understand on first read over the one that sounds knowing to someone who was there.

**Use clear language without watering down the substance.** Write for an intelligent business reader who has not seen the analysis. Assume intelligence, not shared context. Use direct sentences and concrete explanations; keep useful business terminology, meaningful comparisons, and nuance. Explain unfamiliar analytical terms where needed. Do not replace a specific finding with a vague slogan, a childish analogy, or a generic recommendation just to make it simpler. The reader should spend their attention on the implications, not on decoding the phrasing.

**Translate the analysis into a reader's takeaway.** Lead with what we learned about customers or the business and why it matters. Do not use a statistical verdict as a substitute for that explanation. Phrases such as “clears the evidence bar,” “flips under a stricter link rule,” “readable deals,” and “survives correction” belong in the working analysis; explain their consequence in ordinary language where it affects the conclusion. For example, a finding that changes with how conversations are associated with deals is not a dependable basis for choosing a message. State that limitation without making the reader learn the linking algorithm. Preserve the actual population: account-associated evidence must not become a claim that a conversation concerned a particular opportunity.

**Choose the numbers that explain the point.** A summary is not a compressed results table. Start each summary item with one intelligible finding, then include only the numerical comparison needed to understand its scale. Do not stack counts, percentages, gaps, intervals, alternate populations, and sample-size targets into the same sentence or panel. Keep full results in supporting detail. Whenever a number is shown, name what it counts and what happened: “three of eight closed deals were won,” not “three of eight,” “3/8,” or “three for eight” in narrative copy. Explain which deals qualify nearby. A reader should understand the conclusion without doing arithmetic or mapping a sequence of numbers back to a sequence of category names. A table belongs in the main narrative only when comparing its rows is itself the reader's task; otherwise extract the useful finding and put the table in supporting detail.

**State every finding at the level of inference its evidence supports.** Distinguish **observed** (a direct count or sourced fact), **compared** (a difference against a named cohort or baseline), **associated** (co-occurrence without causal proof), and **hypothesized** (a plausible explanation, with alternatives acknowledged). These describe increasing inference, not increasing certainty: a hypothesis is not more certain than an observation. Label explanations as hypotheses and never turn associations into causal claims. These descriptive tools do not establish causation. A causal claim needs a separately justified causal design and evidence; ordinary digest analysis should present candidate mechanisms as hypotheses.

**Preserve meaning when summarizing.** Carry forward the source finding's population, period, denominator, attribution, uncertainty, and material counterevidence. Combining reports or shortening copy must not strengthen a claim. Keep buyer evidence, seller behavior, and approved library strategy separately attributed; seller framing is not buyer reaction, and strategy is not observed fact.

**Be precise about gaps and comparisons.** Absence from the available evidence does not establish absence in the world. Scope absence claims to the sources and period checked. Keep coverage (what was available), confidence (support for a conclusion), and outcome probability separate. For rates, name the numerator and denominator; use counts for small or partially covered samples. If inference is inconclusive, retain valid observed differences with counts and scope; say the evidence does not establish a reliable general difference or equivalence. Name material counterexamples and rival explanations. State well-supported observations plainly; a small sample needs its scope explained, not automatic dismissal.

**Titles are plain observations, not verdicts.** A title that draws a hard line ("demand for X is settled," "buyers have moved on from Y") puts the whole digest out on a limb that one more call can saw off. Prefer the simple observation the evidence carries ("buyers are asking who will run X, not whether to govern it"). The title still states the finding; it does not overclaim it. Cut long or cryptic subtitles: if a lede is not doing clear work under a plain title, the title stands alone.

**Do not manufacture a takeaway.** "No material change," "insufficient evidence," and "worth watching" are complete answers. A section earns prominence through materiality, novelty, persistence, and evidence quality, not through phrasing. Narrow a weakly supported point to its defensible scope; cut it when no useful supported claim remains rather than dressing the evidence up.

## Review

Read the cover, summary, headings, and opening sentences without the methods appendix or prior conversation. Can a reader explain what happened, to whom, and why it matters? Flag undefined ratios, analytical jargon, strings of numbers requiring mental matching, and conclusions buried in tables or footnotes. Simplify by selecting evidence and explaining meaning, while preserving units, population, attribution, and material uncertainty. Pass this check to editorial reviewers. Follow the shared review protocol: reviewers report findings against an immutable version; the author applies changes.

## Analytical spreads

Lead with the takeaway, explain what we heard with useful examples or comparisons, then state what to do only when an action is earned. The working evidence table need not appear. Make the section's question visible, put overall sample context on the opening spread, and keep claim-specific populations and material limitations beside the claims. The closing recommendations must follow from the sections; do not invent new actions for the layout.
