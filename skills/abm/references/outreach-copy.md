# Outreach copy for an account plan (emails and talk tracks)

The email and the talk track are read or heard by the person at the account. Write them the way an experienced rep who knows the product cold writes to someone they respect: specific, concrete, short, in words a person says out loud. The shared [editorial rules](../../shared/editorial-rules.md) apply to every line. These rules come first where they are stricter.

## What the person never reads or hears

- CRM bookkeeping: "closed lost", "no decision", "went dark", "the opportunity", "our CRM shows", "re-engagement", "deal", "pipeline", "stage". Say what happened between people: "we spoke in July", "your team was weighing building it in-house".
- Research worded as surveillance ("I saw in our records", "our notes say").
- Their exact job title ("As VP Finance and Operations, you..."). Refer to their function, team or remit.
- Filler: "I hope this finds you well", "just wanted to reach out", "quick question", "circling back", "touching base".
- A fact that is not on the plan. Never invent a customer, a statistic or an outcome.

## The email

- Line 1 is the greeting alone, then a blank line.
- An email opens on past contact only in two cases. The person was on a call with the team: open on that, in one short sentence. Their team had confirmed two-way contact (a call with a colleague, or a real reply): open on that, with the plain time since ("three months ago", "about a year ago"). Emails that nobody answered are not contact, and an email never recalls them ("as I mentioned", "following up on my note").
- Next, the buying signal, mentioned the way a person would mention it and not as a dated announcement. Then one piece of research about their situation that they would recognize, tied to what they own. Never recite their own product back to them. End on one question.
- 90 to 140 words in the body. Subject line of at most 8 words, plain. Short paragraphs with a blank line between. No sign-off and no signature line.
- Prefer a reference customer whose situation mirrors theirs over an abstract claim, when the library has one that may be named. Never force a poor fit.

## How the signal comes in

- Ease into it with a short lead-in that fits the sentence ("Saw the ... launch", "Looks like ...", "With ... out"). Never open the sentence on the signal as a bare subject: that reads as a press release.
- No calendar dates. If timing helps, say it the way people do ("last month", "this summer").
- The reader may not have worked on the thing that happened. Attribute it to the company or the team that did it, and congratulate only someone who plausibly owned it.
- Lead with what it means for this person's job. It is the reason for writing, not news being reported to them.

## Tying it back to what they told the team

- A thought that draws on past contact opens with a callback that matches how the contact happened. Someone who was on a call: "When we spoke, you mentioned ...". Someone only in an email thread: email words only ("In your note this summer ..."), never "talked" or "spoke". Someone with no direct contact: credit the team ("When we spoke with your team ...").
- Recall the substance in their own terms and keep it concrete: the number, the system, the goal they named.
- Tie the signal to it in the same paragraph: why the news makes what they said matter now.

## How it sounds

- Plain words a person says across a table. No consultant vocabulary.
- Name the sender's product once. Someone who was on a call already knows it: say what is new since then and what that means for the thing they raised. Everyone else gets one plain sentence about the outcome for them.
- Name at most one of their tools, and only one they named to the team.
- End on one easy question they can answer with yes, no, or one of two options, specific to this person. Never an open "what", "which" or "how" question, and never a question about blockers, ownership or approval.

## Across the plan

No two emails share an opener, a lead-in, a callback or a closing question. Each is written for its reader, so a reader who saw two of them would not see a template.

## The talk track

Three beats a rep says out loud: an opener (one or two sentences, the same past-contact opener when the email has one), the value (exactly one sentence on what the product does for this person), and one question. Every beat is script, never advice to the rep.

## Read it back

Read every email and talk track as the recipient would. Fix what does not sound like a person talking and keep what works.

- Check the greeting line, the opener rule, the word count and the single closing question.
- Check who gets the value: what the sender's product does for them is not what their own product does for their customers.
- Keep every concrete anchor (names, numbers, their systems and terms). Never trade a specific for a generic to save words.
- Check again for CRM bookkeeping and for the person's exact title.
