# Meeting-Prep Output Rules

The universal output rules live in the shared layer and are mandatory for this skill:

- [`editorial-rules.md`](../../shared/editorial-rules.md) — language quality, no tool terminology, speculation marking
- [`information-principles.md`](../../shared/information-principles.md) — structure, no redundancy, confirmed-vs-hypothesized tagging, outcome-not-process framing, pain-vs-deal-risk separation
- [`presentation-principles.md`](../../shared/presentation-principles.md) — no text walls, scannability, visual rules

This file adds only the rules specific to meeting prep.

## The Rules

### 1. Every data point gets a label
No orphan values. "$500,000" is meaningless; "Deal Value: $500,000" is information. Every number, date, name, or metric must be accompanied by a label that tells the reader what it is.

### 2. Every section gets a one-line intro
Before presenting content, explain what this section is and why it's here. One sentence at the top of each section that tells the reader what they're looking at and how to use it.

### 3. The header answers three questions
1. What is this document?
2. Who is it about?
3. What's the context?

Not branding, not tool names, not raw metadata. The header is a frame that orients the reader in under 5 seconds.

### 4. Questions fit the decision and meeting

Use grounding diagnostic questions when the workflow is unknown, then consequence and decision probes. Select essential versus optional questions for the purpose and duration; there is no universal L2/L3 requirement or numeric cap. Ground questions in available context and label hypotheses. Recognition can be disconfirmed by the buyer.

### 5. Persona cards are briefings, not dossiers
A persona card tells the seller who this person is, what pressure they're under, and why our product matters to them. That's it. No sub-sections for "How we make them the hero," "Top use cases," "Don't lead with" stacked inside persona cards. Keep each card in the context section to 2-3 rows: Pressure + Why [Product], labeled as hypothesis until the account confirms it. The "What to expect" section is the one place with a longer per-person block (who they are, what they said, what the persona typically raises, the answer that worked, the question to ask), and it does not repeat the context card. Use cases and messaging guidance belong in the messaging or strategy sections.

### 6. Lookalike and expectation claims
Every count names its base ("2 of the 3 won deals with call records"). Comparable-deal findings are told as sequence, never as cause, and carry the word "likely" when they predict this account. Other customers' names appear only in the internal lookalike table and never in wording the rep would say to the buyer. Say how the lookalike set was chosen in its first line.

### 7. State facts, not lookups
Process words ("found", "checked", "not checked", "linked activity", "filed under") do not run through the body. A gap is one plain sentence where it matters ("No reply from us since Mar 19"); "No X found for Y" is fine once where it changes what the rep does, not as a refrain; what was not checked goes once in the sources footer. The asset line in the keep-going block (asset UUID and URL, or the deal id, domain and date when unpublished) is an identifier line and is exempt from the id rule. Required wording stays: "not checked" in a lookalike table cell, "no calls linked" in a row, "of the N calls checked" on a count, and naming the deal an account's calls are attached to.

The reader sees the fact with its date or source in plain words. No "returned", "retrieved", "the graph", "the data shows", "I opened", "I read", "I counted", tool or field names, or ids as prose, in any heading, table or note. Ids and tool names appear only as links or in the keep-going prompts, which are written for an agent.

### 8. Next best action has four parts
The move, why now, strategy source, follow-through. A move without all four is cut or turned into a question inside another move. Zero moves is stated plainly.

### 9. Content and framework are real
Recommend only content, slides, proof and frameworks you saw in the library or resources, named as they appear (a verbatim title or quote is exempt from the word rules). The framework treatment appears only when one exists.

### 10. Plain language
Short sentences, concrete pictures from the account, ordinary sales words left unexplained. No em dashes. No contrast constructions in prose ("X, not Y"). Do not use: leverage, robust, comprehensive, seamless, actionable, impactful, landscape, unpack, deep dive, best practices. Sub-headings, table headers and card titles state a finding or action in one clear clause (the eight section titles are fixed by meeting-sections.md). Never write "library", "entity", "concept graph", "outcome stats" or "approved answer" as a term on the page (write "on file" or name what the record says; these words are fine in these instructions); say what the strategy says in plain words. Ordinary domain words (coverage, match, mention) stay when they are the buyer's or the seller's own vocabulary, and a verbatim quote is never altered to satisfy a word rule.
